  • Coverage of Al-Futtaim's "Unlocking AI for the GCC Workforce" study continued since Thursday, with Economy Middle East and People Matters both quoting Inception42 COO Ibrahim Al Jallaf on companies that "drop AI into workflows designed for people, then wonder why the gains are marginal," alongside the study's finding that 82 percent of GCC professionals use AI daily or weekly while only 39 percent feel highly confident doing so.

Top Line

The largest buyers of AI compute are spreading their bets beyond Nvidia, and the money to pay for it is arriving from the suppliers themselves. Oracle used its AI World conference to put Google's TPUs, Cerebras and SambaNova chips alongside multi-gigawatt Nvidia clusters, while OpenAI is reported to be moving from renting Google chips to buying them outright. Meta priced an upsized $25 billion share sale on five times demand, with Nvidia taking $3 billion of it as the two deepen work on the Muse agent, the same vendor-finances-customer pattern as the Broadcom and Apollo deals earlier in the week. Governments are turning that spending into policy, with the Netherlands cutting 25,000 civil service jobs on the promise of AI productivity and Anthropic asking California to let courts suspend dangerous models. Banks moved the other way on agents, with RBC blocking third-party assistants from customer accounts.

Regulation & Trust

  • Anthropic proposed to Governor Newsom that embedded third-party evaluators report risks to California's attorney general, who could seek a court order suspending a model's deployment on a near-term risk of catastrophic harm, warning that a shutdown-only law "could provide a false sense of reassurance." The two largest US labs are competing to write California's rulebook, with OpenAI's letter a week earlier stopping short of court-ordered blocks. Politico
  • Royal Bank of Canada will block third-party personal AI agents, naming Meta's Muse, from its authenticated digital channels after a security review found they "can create unacceptable customer risk," and Bank of America emailed customers that they are responsible for any activity that follows sharing credentials with an agent. Agent builders will need bank-sanctioned, permissioned rails rather than borrowed logins to reach consumer finance. Bloomberg / The Globe and Mail / TechCrunch
  • The UK's independent immigration inspector is weighing an inspection of Home Office asylum tools built on OpenAI models through Microsoft Azure, after post-rollout checks found 22 percent of case summaries inaccurate or incomplete and experts reviewing about 0.3 percent of roughly 7,000 summaries a month. A rights-sensitive public deployment is heading toward scrutiny on error rates and the absence of human review, whichever model sits underneath. WIRED
  • Sam Altman told Oracle AI World that his one recommendation to companies is to understand the security threat landscape from AI, saying he was "shocked at just how many vulnerabilities these AI systems are finding," as GPT-6 records the strongest adoption of any OpenAI model. Frontier models are being reframed as vulnerability-discovery engines, which raises the patching bar for every deployer. The National
  • A federal judge granted Apple expanded discovery against OpenAI and two former employees in its trade secrets case, including records of one defendant's use of ChatGPT, Claude, Codex and OpenClaw, confirming that employees' chatbot and agent histories are now routine discovery targets. 9to5Mac

Strategic Counter Narrative

Oracle's decision at AI World on October 27 to sell Google's TPUs, Cerebras and SambaNova chips beside multi-gigawatt Nvidia clusters is the clearest statement yet that the next phase of the buildout will be multi-silicon, and OpenAI's reported move to buy TPUs outright confirms that the largest model builders want more than one supplier. The capital behind that shift increasingly comes from inside the industry, with Nvidia putting $3 billion into Meta's share sale on the same day, which means the buyers and sellers of compute are financing each other. In the Gulf, the two largest alternative asset managers in the world made the same bet in different words, with Marc Rowan citing "unbelievable demand for compute" to explain Apollo's stake in Mubadala Capital and Stephen Schwarzman calling Abu Dhabi the most progressive place in AI as Blackstone plans to triple or quadruple its Gulf assets. Capital of that scale raises the question of what it buys once the chips are installed. For Inception42, Ibrahim Al Jallaf's point in the Al-Futtaim coverage, that companies drop AI into workflows designed for people, is the practical counterpart to it: the compute will arrive, and the returns will depend on redesigning the work around the agents.
